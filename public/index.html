Skyline GT-R History

Forty-three years of Skyline before this archive's first record, the Nismo Omori Factory special editions, and the tuner shops that shaped how the world sees the car.

Unlike the rest of the archive, this section is curated general history, not derived from the FAST records. Approximate figures are worded as approximate, and every claim here was checked against public sources before writing.

The Lineage

Six decades of Skyline, condensed to the moments that actually shaped the badge.

May 1964

Before The Badge: A Skyline Chases a Porsche

There was no "GT-R" yet — just the Prince Skyline, built by Prince Motor Company before its 1966 merger into Nissan. To homologate a race version for the 2nd Japan Grand Prix, Prince's engineers stretched a Skyline 1500's nose by 200mm and shoehorned in the straight-six "G7" engine from the larger Gloria, hand-building the run in the two days before the race.

It worked well enough that driver Yoshikazu Sunako briefly passed the race's favourite, a factory Porsche 904 Carrera GTS, before the German car reclaimed the lead and the win. Second place wasn't a victory, but a Japanese sedan running with a purpose-built European sports car was enough to make the country pay attention — the moment every Skyline GT-R since has traced itself back to.

1969–1972

The Hakosuka (PGC10 / KPGC10)

The GT-R badge itself began in February 1969, on the four-door PGC10, joined a year later by a lighter two-door version, the KPGC10 — nicknamed Hakosuka ("boxy Skyline") by fans ever since. Both carried the S20: a 160 PS DOHC 24-valve straight-six directly related to the GR8 racing engine out of Prince's own R380 prototype, in a chassis that otherwise had no business hosting a race motor.

It worked. The Hakosuka went on a 50-race winning streak in Japanese touring car racing between 1969 and 1972 — the result that actually built the GT-R legend, years before anyone outside Japan had heard the name.

1973

The Kenmeri (KPGC110)

The second-generation GT-R arrived in January 1973 on the C110 Skyline, nicknamed Kenmeri after an ad campaign built around a couple named Ken and Mary — a softer, more road-going take on the formula than the racing-bred Hakosuka. It never got the chance to prove itself: the 1973 oil crisis and Japan's newly tightened emissions rules made the high-strung S20 impossible to keep selling, and Nissan cancelled the car within the year.

Only 197 were built before the badge disappeared entirely. The Kenmeri predates every chassis this archive holds real FAST VIN records for — it isn't part of the VIN Database, so this figure is public production history, not something pulled from this site's own records.

1973–1989

Sixteen Years Without a GT-R

No Skyline wore the GT-R badge for the next sixteen years. It's not that Nissan stopped building fast Skylines — the R30 generation's DR30 ("Tekkamen," the Iron Mask, for its blacked-out grille) ran a turbocharged FJ20ET four-cylinder and was homologated into Group A racing in the early '80s, and the R31's HR31 GTS-R that followed in late 1987 built 823 homologation cars around a reworked, bigger-turbo RB20DET specifically to win Group A touring car racing. Both were genuinely quick factory Nissans. Neither got to call itself a GT-R.

Both of those chassis exist in the same Nissan FAST source this archive reads from — the DR30 and HR31 files hold over 220,000 combined factory records — but they're deliberately left out of this build, since the site draws its line at the GT-R badge itself.

1989–1994

R32: Godzilla Returns

The badge returned in 1989 on the BNR32, and this time it came with technology no one else in touring car racing had answered yet: the RB26DETT twin-turbo straight-six and ATTESA E-TS computer-controlled all-wheel drive. The result went undefeated through the JTCC and dominated Australian and Japanese Group A racing so completely that regulators eventually rewrote the rules just to stop it — the run that earned it the "Godzilla" nickname from Wheels magazine in 1989.

1995–1998

R33: The Nürburgring Record

The BCNR33 kept the RB26DETT and widened what ATTESA E-TS Pro could do, and in 1995 became the first production car to lap the Nürburgring Nordschleife in under 8 minutes — 7:59.887, a record this archive's own BCNR33 entry still carries. Nismo followed it with two of the rarest road-going GT-Rs ever built: the 400R (a 2.8-litre stroker RB-X GT2 rated at 400 PS, only 44 built of a planned 100) and the LM Limited (188 cars in Champion Blue, marking Nismo's 1995–96 Le Mans GT class entry).

1999–2002

R34: The Last of the Inline-Six GT-Rs

The BNR34 was the final Skyline GT-R built around the RB26DETT, in a shorter, stiffer body with a Getrag 6-speed and the industry's first factory colour multi-function LCD display, tracking boost and oil temperature in the centre stack. Nissan split it into four real trims over the run — base, V-Spec (active LSD, carbon bonnet, upgraded turbos), V-Spec II (2000, stiffer again, NACA-duct bonnet, ball-bearing turbos) and M-Spec (2001, leather and a Bose stereo over outright stiffness) — before closing the RB26 era out with the Nür in 2002: 1,003 final-run cars (718 V-Spec II Nür, 285 M-Spec Nür) built around N1 racing-spec internals.

It's also the only generation Nismo chose for its most extreme factory tuning programmes — see Omori Factory Special Editions below — and, through 2 Fast 2 Furious and Gran Turismo, the generation that introduced the GT-R to an entire generation of enthusiasts who'd never see one drive past in person.

2007

R35: A New Name

When the GT-R returned in 2007, Nissan dropped "Skyline" from the name and gave it a new VR38DETT twin-turbo V6 on a purpose-built platform — a clean break from the RB26 lineage this archive documents, not an evolution of it. It's a different car built for a different era, which is why it isn't part of this archive.

Omori Factory Special Editions

Nismo's own three-tier factory tuning programme for the RB26DETT — S, R and Z, in ascending order of how far Nismo was willing to go. All three grew out of Nismo's Omori facility, the same shop that still runs Nissan's official heritage restoration work today.

S-tune

"Street" — the road-biased tier

~14cars converted
~350 PStypical output

Nismo's most road-focused build: an aggressive camshaft and a metal head gasket, with boost raised to roughly 1.1 bar — aimed at everyday drivability and throttle response rather than a lap time. Exhaust ran through Nismo's own NE-1 muffler, engineered with an isometric header layout from each turbo outlet back to the collector so all six cylinders pulse evenly rather than fight each other. The least extreme of the three tiers and the rarest to find offered today — dyno'd examples generally land in the 330–350 PS band, with some variance depending on exactly which options a given car left the shop with.

R-tune

The race-leaning tier

~75–80cars built
~450 PSR1 target output

A genuinely deeper build: N1-spec internals — block, pistons, crank — the same cylinder block later used in the BNR34 Nür, plus a thinner head gasket for higher compression, boost up to 1.2 bar, and the R1 engine package (officially "R-tune Concept Engine Spec 1") on top of an upgraded turbo, camshaft, intercooler, radiator, oil cooler and twin-plate clutch, targeting around 450 PS / 50 kgm. R-tune conversions themselves ran across the BNR33, BCNR33 and BNR34; Nismo's parts catalog later extended the standalone R1 engine kit to the BNR32 as well, making it the one Omori spec you can still buy into on any of the four RB26 GT-R generations.

Z-tune

The ultimate tier — R34 only

20ever completed
500 PS / 55 kgmofficial rating

The most extreme of the three, and R34-exclusive. Nismo bought back 20 low-mileage, damage-free V-Spec GT-Rs with under 30,000 km on the clock, stripped them to the bare chassis, reinforced the shell with carbon fibre, and rebuilt the RB26 as a 2.8-litre stroker officially named "Z2" — a different engine to the 400R's RB-X GT2 a decade earlier, despite the similar spec sheet. Per Nismo's own published build sheet, the Z2 borrows a GT500-race-derived crankshaft (77.7mm stroke, 4mm over stock) and 121.5mm SNCM439 connecting rods, runs on an 87mm-bore block, breathes through a ball-bearing IHI turbo shared with Nismo's Nürburgring 24-Hour programme, and exhales through titanium piping; every piston and rod was weight-matched within a gram, every crank dynamically balanced, and all 20 engines carry their own individual serial number. Built to mark Nismo's 20th anniversary after a 2003 Nürburgring test programme, it remains the single most valuable R34 GT-R variant, trading today well into seven figures.

Famous Tuner Builds

The independent Japanese shops that built their own reputations on the GT-R, working alongside — not for — Nissan's own factory programmes.

Top Secret

Kazuhiko "Smokey" Nagata — Chiba, founded 1991

650 hpgold "650R" R32 build
2019Best Demo Car, Tokyo Auto Salon

Nagata's tuning work traces back to 1978, working discreetly out of Trust; "Top Secret" became its own name, then its own shop in Chiba, in 1991. Its signature build across the RB26 generations is the gold "650R" — an R32 GT-R stroked to 2.8 litres with HKS pistons and crank, Nismo bearings and Tomei camshafts, running a single GReddy T78 turbo in place of the stock twin-turbo pair, for 650 hp under Top Secret's own carbon aero kit. It won Best Demo Car at the 2019 Tokyo Auto Salon before selling to a US collector. Nagata's wider fame, though, actually traces to a different car: a 1,003 hp Mk IV Toyota Supra fitted with an RB26DETT swap, which he drove to a reported 194–197 mph on the UK's A1(M) motorway in November 1998, chasing an even 200 mph before wet roads and a police stop cut the run short — he spent the night in a UK jail and was banned from the country for a decade.

Mine's

Michizo Niikura — Yokosuka, founded 1985

1988first ECU tune
57.7sTsukuba lap

Founded in 1985 in Yokosuka, Mine's built its early reputation on ECU work, launching the VX-ROM reprogrammed ECU for the R31 GT-R in 1988 — among the first aftermarket ECU tunes sold for any Japanese sports car. Its philosophy stayed distinct from rivals chasing bigger displacement: where Top Secret's 650R and Nismo's own Z-tune reached for 2.8-litre strokers, Mine's "Spec 2" RB26 build for its BNR34 kept the stock 2.6-litre capacity and leaned on balance instead — a Tomei-sourced crank, H-beam rods and forged pistons matched to tight tolerances, twin HKS GT2530 turbos, and cams mild enough (260° intake, 252° exhaust) to idle cleanly on the street while still spinning well past 9,000 rpm. That combination lapped Tsukuba Circuit in 57.7 seconds — a refined, road-registered car rather than a stripped-out time-attack special.

TommyKaira

Yoshikazu Tomita & Kikuo Kaira — Kyoto, founded 1986

95R32 "R" built
16R33 "Full Spec R" built

Founded in 1986 — the name is a portmanteau of its two founders — TommyKaira built its first Skyline tune on the R31 GTS-R in 1988, then followed the GT-R through the R32, R33 and R34, offering Look, Basic and Full Spec R packages depending on how far a customer wanted to go. Its R32-based flagship was simply named "R": a wide-body kit, three-piece alloy wheels, upgraded turbos, a 3-inch exhaust, a front strut brace and Bilstein dampers pushed output to roughly 345–350 hp, in a run of only 95 cars. The R33 generation's rarest variant, "Full Spec R," went further still — just 16 were ever built. Full Spec R33s and R34s are still traded today specifically for the TommyKaira badge.

Skyline Authenticity & Identification Guide

Spotting genuine models, verifying firewall stamped chassis numbers, and identifying fake GT-R conversions from ER34 GT-T or ECR33 donors.

01
Blue Model Identification Plate

Located on the passenger side firewall. The plate is made of blue anodized stamped aluminum attached with two round factory blind rivets. Ensure the font is embossed from behind, with no signs of rivet tampering or laser etching.

02
Firewall Stamped Chassis Number

Directly stamped into the metal firewall above the brake booster (e.g. BNR34-001234 or ER34-024890). Factory stamps feature distinct serif font styles and uniform stamping depth with undisturbed factory paint.

03
Spotting GT-T to GT-R Conversions

Key indicators: Genuine GT-Rs feature factory widened rear steel quarters (no body filler seams), wide aluminum front fenders with GT-R badges, 4WD front driveshaft tunnel clearance, factory ATTESA pump in the trunk, and genuine MFD wiring loom.

04
Engine Block Casting Numbers

Standard RB26DETT features casting mark 05U on the intake side. N1 homologation and Nür models feature the reinforced thick-wall 24U casting. RB25DET NEO features NEO stamped on the cam covers and high-compression solid lifters.

Why is the Nissan Skyline GT-R called "Godzilla"?

In July 1989, Australian motoring magazine Wheels published a cover feature on the revolutionary new BNR32 Skyline GT-R titled "Godzilla: The Monster from Japan".

Equipped with its all-conquering RB26DETT twin-turbo engine and computerized ATTESA E-TS all-wheel drive, the GT-R dominated Australian Group A touring car racing. Driven by Jim Richards and Mark Skaife for Gibson Motorsport, the R32 won the prestigious Bathurst 1000 in 1991 and 1992, and back-to-back Australian Touring Car Championships (ATCC).

In Japan, the BNR32 achieved an astonishing 29 victories out of 29 races (29-0) in the Japanese Touring Car Championship (JTCC) across four seasons, remaining completely undefeated until the series disbanded. The car was so dominant that Australian racing authorities abolished Group A rules entirely to ban the GT-R from competition.
